How it works

Four steps, fifteen times a second.

Lanefinder needs no beacons and no changes to the pool. It reads the markings every pool already has, and everything runs on the goggles themselves.

01

See

A wide-angle camera in the bridge watches the pool floor and finds the dark line that runs down the middle of every lane.

02

Steady

The motion sensor knows when you turn your head to breathe and skips those frames. Between good frames, the gyroscope holds your heading.

03

Decide

A small neural network measures how far you are from the line and at what angle, and spots the T-mark 2 m before the wall. A filter blends it with motion data 100 times a second.

04

Feel

Drift, and the temple on that side taps. Near the wall, both sides count down three taps. Then one long tap means turn.

Silence means you're straight.

No beeps and no voice over your music. The goggles only speak up when you need to change something.
Haptic language

A vocabulary of taps, learned in one lap.

The side that taps is the side you are drifting toward, like the rumble strip at the edge of a road. Steer away from the taps.

On the lineLeftSilentRightSilentYou're within 15 cm of your target line.
Drifting leftLeftRightSilent15–35 cm off. Slow taps, one about every 0.6 s.
Far leftLeftRightSilentMore than 35 cm off. Fast taps until you correct.
Drifting rightLeftSilentRightThe same, mirrored. Taps get faster the further you drift.

In a shared lane, swimmers keep to one side of the line. Set the target 60 cm to the right of it in the app, and the same taps keep you on your side.

Inside

Four sensors in. Two senses out.

Every decision is made on the goggles. The phone app is only used on dry land, to set up the goggles, load music and read back your swims.

Why it's buildable

Built on rules pools and swimmers already follow.

2.0 m

Every competition pool marks the wall.

World Aquatics rules put a dark line, 20–30 cm wide, down the middle of each lane. It ends 2.0 m before the wall in a cross-bar 1 m long. Lanefinder reads that T as its wall warning.

≈ 5 cm

Bluetooth stops at the surface.

Water absorbs 2.4 GHz radio within a few centimeters, so nothing can stream during a swim. Music lives on the goggles, the way waterproof swim headphones already work.

S11

Black lenses are already the rule.

Paralympic swimmers in the S11 class must race in blackened goggles. Opaque cups lose nothing. Clear lenses swap in for swimmers with low vision.

Temple

Heart rate there is proven.

Polar sells a goggle clip that puts its optical sensor on the temple, and FORM's smart swim goggles measure there too. The temporal artery sits just under the skin.

Tapper

It does the tapping coach's job.

Blind swimmers train with a coach at the wall who taps them with a pole before each turn. Lanefinder gives the same cue, so they can train without one.

Hard problems, planned for.

Bubbles, splash and glare hide the line

The network is trained on footage full of bubbles, light patterns and glare. Frames below 70% confidence are dropped, and the gyroscope holds heading until a good frame returns.

Turning the head to breathe

The motion sensor flags head roll above 25°. Those frames are skipped, so the line never seems to jump.

Another swimmer covers the line

Shapes that move against the floor are masked out. If the line stays hidden for 3 s, a double tap says guidance is holding heading on the gyroscope.

Backstroke

Face up, the same camera sees the ceiling. It spots the backstroke flags hung 5 m from each wall, and the gyroscope keeps you straight in between.

Shared lanes

The target is set in the app: on the line for a private lane, or 60 cm to its right for circle swimming.

Fogging

The camera sits behind a flat sapphire window in a sealed chamber filled with dry nitrogen. The lens cups get an anti-fog coating.

Water getting in

No open ports. The electronics are potted, the pods are welded shut, the pressure sensor is gel-filled, and the charging contacts are switched off unless a charger is detected.

Battery life

About 2 hours on the prototype. A dedicated AI chip such as the MAX78000 cuts the power drawn by the camera and network several times over, for a 5 hour target.

Prototype plan

From bench to pool in 14 weeks.

Every part is available off the shelf. The first working goggles can be built with a 3D printer, a soldering iron and about $180 in parts.

Weeks 1–3

1 · Bench

Film the pool floor from goggle height in three pools. Label the frames, train the line network on a laptop, then run it on the ESP32-S3.

Weeks 4–6

2 · Dry box

All electronics in a sealed clear box on a standard goggle strap. Tune the tap patterns with blindfolded swimmers in a shallow lane.

Weeks 7–10

3 · Integrated

Resin-printed frame and pods, flex harness, potted electronics. Leak-tested for 2 hours at the bottom of a 3 m dive pool.

Weeks 11–14

4 · Pool trials

Sessions with blind club swimmers and their coach, comparing Lanefinder against a human tapper and against no guidance.

< 1

False alarm per 100 m, counted from video of every trial session.
